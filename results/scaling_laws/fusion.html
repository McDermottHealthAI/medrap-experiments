How fusion works, and whether it loses the patient

Fusion is the step where the patient model "reads" each retrieved document. This page walks through it with the exact shapes from our code (PerDocCrossAttentionFusion, MedRAP c3700ab), then answers your question: does the patient information get lost there?

Short answer

The patient information is not cut off: it flows through the fusion on a "residual" path, and the documents are added on top. But the design weakens it in four ways (section 4). The two most concrete are that the final average includes padding positions (a third of all positions on average) and that the fusion is a fixed ~1.85M-parameter block that dwarfs the patient encoder at most sizes we tested.

3. Step by step: what comes in, what comes out

  1. Copy the patient once per document. Each document is read separately, so the patient's 256 code vectors are duplicated K times.
    in (32, 256, 64) → out (160, 256, 64)  ·  160 = 32 patients × 5 documents
  2. Project the patient to the fusion width. A linear layer maps every code vector from W to 256 numbers. For W ≤ 256 this can't lose information (it only stretches); at W = 4 it turns 4 numbers into 256.
    in (160, 256, W) → out (160, 256, 256)  ·  W·256 + 256 parameters
  3. Two cross-attention layers. Each patient position asks "which words of this document matter to me?", gathers a weighted mix of the document's word vectors, and adds it to its own vector. A small feed-forward network then reworks each vector and adds that too. Because every step is an addition onto the patient's own vector (a residual connection), the patient signal is carried forward, not replaced.
    patient in (160, 256, 256) + doc (160, 256, 1024) → out (160, 256, 256)  ·  ≈922K parameters per layer
  4. Normalize. LayerNorm rescales each position's vector.
    (160, 256, 256) → (160, 256, 256)
  5. Average over the patient's positions. One vector per (patient, document) pair. This average runs over all 256 positions, padding included (see 4a).
    in (160, 256, 256) → out (32, 5, 256)
  6. Predict once per document. The head (linear, 256 → T) gives a score for every task, separately for each document.
    in (32, 5, 256) → out (32, 5, 625)
  7. Average the per-document predictions. Weights come from query · key (softmax over the 5 documents). Final probability per task: p = Σk wk σ(zk).
    in (32, 5, 625) → out (32, 625)

Patient-only model, for comparison

Encoder output (32, 256, W) → masked mean over real codes only → (32, W) → head W → T → (32, 625). The head sees the encoder's summary directly.

Retrieval model

The head never sees the encoder's summary directly. It sees the patient only after projection, two rounds of mixing with a document, normalization, and an unmasked average.

4. Is patient information being lost?

Not cut off, but weakened. Four concrete reasons, from the code and our parameter counts:

4a. Padding is averaged in (step 5)

Patients have up to 256 codes; shorter histories are padded. On the test set a patient has 171 real codes on average, so 33% of positions are padding (logged as mask/test/pad_fraction = 0.333). The model passes the padding mask to the fusion (patient_attention_mask), but PerDocCrossAttentionFusion ignores it and takes a plain x.mean(dim=1). The patient-only model uses a masked mean.

Padding positions start from a zero embedding but still attend to the patient's codes in the encoder, and in the fusion they attend to the document like any other position. So for a patient with a short history, most of the averaged vector comes from positions that carry a blurred copy of the patient plus document content, and the real per-code detail is diluted.

4b. No direct path from the patient summary to the head

In patient-only the head reads the encoder summary. In retrieval it reads a vector that went through two mixing layers with dropout 0.1 and LayerNorm. The residual connections keep the patient signal in principle, but the model has to learn to protect it while also learning to use documents.

4c. The fusion is a fixed, large block

The fusion always works at width 256, whatever W is. Its size from our parameter counts:

So at most widths the retrieval model's capacity is mostly the fusion, and growing the patient encoder changes the total little. That matches P2, where retrieval barely improves with encoder size. It also explains why retrieval wins only at the tiniest widths: there it adds ~1.8M parameters to a 180-parameter model, and the control (#4, random documents) wins there too, so the gain is capacity, not knowledge.

4d. Every prediction is made "through" a document

The head predicts separately for each of the K documents, and the final answer is a weighted average. The patient is present in every per-document vector, so averaging doesn't remove it, but each vector is also shaped by its document. P7 shows the models come to depend on that: swapping in a random passage at test raises the loss of #2 in 1,133 of 1,200 runs, even though random passages during training (#4) work just as well. The model leans on the document channel without gaining knowledge from it.

5. How this fits our results

P2 is flat for retrieval

Expected if the fixed fusion, not the encoder, sets the retrieval model's capacity (4c), and if per-code detail is diluted (4a).

#2 ≈ #4

Real documents add nothing over random ones: the documents act as an extra channel, not as knowledge.

Gap grows with scale

Patient-only keeps improving with W and D; the retrieval path caps how much patient detail reaches the head (4a, 4b).

These are explanations consistent with the code and the results, not yet tested causes.
